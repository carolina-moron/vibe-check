# Voices: first-person scripts for AI avatar videos

Short first-person monologues, one for each type of scam and exploitation that Vibe Check covers. They are written to be spoken by an AI avatar in a GIF or short video, so people can feel what these situations are like from the inside.

## Read this before producing anything

- **These are not real people.** Every narrator is a composite. Nothing here is a quote from a real victim, and no avatar should look like, or be named after, a real victim, defendant or public figure.
- **The facts are real.** Scripts marked *Based on a case* use only facts from that case's public sources (in `data/cases/`). Scripts marked *Based on official guidance* describe a typical pattern from the FTC, the FBI or UNODC, not one specific person.
- **Label every video as AI.** Show a visible "AI presenter, composite story" badge, as Avatar Impact Stories does. Put the "Based on" line in the caption or description.
- **Cast with care.** Match the setting and language to the story's context, but avoid stereotypes. Don't make victims look foolish or ashamed. The narrator is someone who was deceived by professionals.
- **Keep it non-graphic.** Describe violence and sexual exploitation, but don't show or dramatise it.
- **End on help.** Close each video with the takeaway line and "Something feels off? Check the vibe" plus a hotline card. US National Human Trafficking Hotline: 1-888-373-7888 (text 233733). UK Modern Slavery Helpline: 08000 121 700.

Each script runs about 45 to 75 seconds when spoken (roughly 120 to 180 words).

## 1. Fake job abroad that ends in a scam compound

**Based on a case:** KK Park and the Myawaddy scam compounds, Myanmar, 2022–2025.
**Avatar brief:** a young adult speaking in a quiet room, calm but tired. Their voice drops when they describe the compound.

> The job offer came in a message. Good pay in Thailand, flights paid, housing included. I didn't check anything, because there was nothing to check — just a person in a chat, being friendly.
>
> Someone met me in Bangkok and drove me north for hours. We crossed a river near Mae Sot, and only then did I understand we were in Myanmar. Walls. Guards. They took my phone and my passport.
>
> My new job was to message strangers online and convince them to invest in fake platforms. If I didn't reach my targets, I was punished.
>
> The worst part is that I became the friendly person in the chat. The same thing that trapped me, I was forced to do to others.
>
> If a job abroad arranges everything for you and won't tell you the exact address — stop. Ask someone you trust to look it up with you.

**Takeaway on screen:** A job that won't tell you where you'll work isn't a job.
**Facts from:** US Treasury sanctions press releases (2025-05-05 and 2025-09-08); UN News (2023-08-29); RFA (2025-01-10). See `data/cases/kk-park-shwe-kokko-myawaddy.json`.

## 2. Labour trafficking through a work visa

**Based on a case:** Signal International, Indian H-2B workers, 2006–2015.
**Avatar brief:** a man in his thirties or forties in work clothes, speaking steadily, sometimes proud, sometimes angry.

> I'm a welder. After Hurricane Katrina, recruiters told us American shipyards needed skilled men. And they promised something bigger than a job: a green card. A future for my family.
>
> The fees were ten, twenty thousand dollars. My family sold what we had and borrowed the rest.
>
> In Mississippi I learned my visa was temporary. There was no green card. We lived in a guarded camp, many men to a room, and they took over a thousand dollars a month from our pay for it.
>
> I couldn't go home. The debt was waiting there.
>
> Some of us went to court. In 2015 a jury said what happened to us was labour trafficking.
>
> If a recruiter asks you to pay thousands for a job and promises papers they can't show you — that's the trap, not the opportunity.

**Takeaway on screen:** Real employers don't make you pay to work.
**Facts from:** SPLC (2015); EEOC (2015). See `data/cases/signal-international.json`.

## 3. Farm work, passports taken

**Based on a case:** Los Villatoros Harvesting, Mexican H-2A farmworkers, 2015–2017.
**Avatar brief:** a farmworker outdoors at the end of the day, speaking in Spanish with subtitles if possible.

> In Mexico they told me: legal farm work in the United States. Fair hours, good pay, they'd pay me back for travel. I only had to pay the recruitment fees first.
>
> When we arrived, they kept our passports "for safekeeping." Then the debt started. Every week I owed more.
>
> We worked in the fields six, seven days a week. They moved us from state to state — Florida, Kentucky, Georgia. We slept crowded in dirty rooms. If anyone complained, they said they'd call immigration, or that our families back home would pay for it.
>
> The men who did this went to prison. But I want you to know the signs before it happens to you.
>
> Nobody gets to keep your passport. Not your boss, not your recruiter. Nobody.

**Takeaway on screen:** Your passport is yours. Always.
**Facts from:** US Department of Justice (2026-06-09). See `data/cases/los-villatoros-harvesting-h2a-forced-labor.json`.

## 4. Domestic servitude

**Based on a case:** Bolaji Bolarinwa, New Jersey, 2015–2016.
**Avatar brief:** a woman in a kitchen or small room, speaking quietly, as if she is still getting used to being heard.

> She promised me a chance to come to America. I would study, and help a little in her home.
>
> When I arrived, she took my passport. From early morning until late at night I cleaned, cooked and cared for her children. There was almost no pay.
>
> I wasn't allowed to go out. I wasn't allowed to talk to people. She watched me all the time. When I did something wrong, she shouted, she hit me, and she threatened my daughter.
>
> From outside, it looked like a family with a helper. Nobody saw it.
>
> In 2025 a court sentenced her to prison for forced labour.
>
> If someone who brought you to a country holds your documents and won't let you leave the house, it is not your fault, and there are people who will help you.

**Takeaway on screen:** Forced labour can happen inside an ordinary home.
**Facts from:** US Department of Justice (2025-05-09). See `data/cases/bolarinwa-moorestown-domestic-servitude.json`.

## 5. Sex trafficking that starts with a promise of money

**Based on cases:** Delon Smith (Florida, 2021–2022) and Brandon Sartor (multi-state, 2024–2025).
**Avatar brief:** a young woman in soft light, framed from the shoulders up, speaking slowly. Nothing graphic.

> He said he could help me make money. I was struggling, and he seemed like he had a plan.
>
> At first he was generous. Then the rules started. Then the threats. He hurt me, and he said he'd hurt my family if I left. He took all the money. He moved me from city to city so I never knew anyone.
>
> People ask why I didn't just walk away. When someone controls your money, your phone, where you sleep, and you're afraid for your family — there's nowhere to walk to.
>
> Men like him have been sentenced to decades in prison. But it starts long before a courtroom. It starts with "I can help you make money."
>
> If someone's help comes with secrets, control and fear, it isn't help. You can call the hotline — you don't need proof.

**Takeaway on screen:** "I can help you make money" can be the first step of a trap.
**Facts from:** US Department of Justice press releases on Delon Richard Smith (sentenced 2026) and Brandon Sartor (guilty plea 2026). See `data/cases/delon-smith-daytona-sex-trafficking.json` and `data/cases/brandon-sartor-multistate-sex-trafficking.json`.

## 6. Recruited abroad for a "work-study" programme

**Based on a case:** Alabuga Start, Russia.
**Avatar brief:** a young woman of about 20 with a suitcase or airport backdrop, excited at the start, flat and serious by the end.

> I saw it on social media. A work-study programme abroad — hospitality, catering. Plane ticket paid, a salary, a chance to see the world. I was nineteen.
>
> When I arrived, the job was not hospitality. We were put on long shifts building attack drones. We were watched all the time. The pay was not what they promised. Some of the girls got sick from the chemicals.
>
> My parents were proud of me when I left. I didn't know how to tell them.
>
> Governments have since sanctioned the place I was sent.
>
> If a programme recruits young people on social media, pays for everything and is vague about the actual work, ask to see a contract, talk to people who came back, and check the name in the news.

**Takeaway on screen:** Check what the job actually is before you get on the plane.
**Facts from:** US Treasury (2024); AP investigation as summarised in the case record. See `data/cases/alabuga-start.json`.

## 7. Fake recruiter who charges for interviews

**Based on a case:** FTC v. Worldwide Executive Job Search Solutions, 2019.
**Avatar brief:** a professional in their forties or fifties in a home office, a little embarrassed but determined to warn others.

> I had twenty years of experience and I'd been out of work for months. Then a recruiter messaged me on LinkedIn: exclusive executive roles at private equity firms, jobs no one else could see.
>
> To get the interview, I had to pay a fee. Almost two thousand dollars. Then they offered to fix my résumé — another fee.
>
> I told myself that's how things work at that level.
>
> The interviews went nowhere. The jobs didn't exist. The Federal Trade Commission shut them down and banned them from ever selling job services again.
>
> I'm not naive. I was hopeful. That's what they were counting on.
>
> Real recruiters are paid by the company, not by you. If you have to pay to get an interview, walk away.

**Takeaway on screen:** You should never pay for a job interview.
**Facts from:** FTC press releases (2019-02-25 and 2019-10-08). See `data/cases/worldwide-executive-job-search-solutions.json`.

## 8. Work-from-home "pay to start" scam

**Based on a case:** Mattox and Hudson booklet scheme, Georgia (charges only).
**Avatar brief:** a parent at a kitchen table with a laptop, speaking warmly and with some humour.

> Twenty dollars per booklet. Up to five thousand dollars a week. From home, around my kids' schedule. I wanted to believe it so badly.
>
> There was a small initiation fee, paid by money order. I sent it.
>
> The materials never came. People who did get them and did the work never got paid. Later I found the same offer under more than a dozen different company names.
>
> More than two hundred people paid. Federal prosecutors charged two people over it.
>
> Here's what I tell my friends now: if a job asks you to pay before you earn anything, it's not a job — you're the customer.

**Takeaway on screen:** If you pay to start, you're the customer, not the employee.
**Facts from:** US Attorney's Office, Northern District of Georgia (2012-08-21). These are charges; no outcome was verified. See `data/cases/mattox-hudson-work-from-home-booklet-scheme.json`.

## 9. Reshipping and money mule jobs

**Based on official guidance:** FBI IC3 money mule warning; FTC job scams guidance.
**Avatar brief:** a student or young adult surrounded by cardboard boxes, confused and then worried.

> The job was called "logistics assistant." Work from home. I'd receive packages, check them, take off the original labels and ship them to new addresses. Easy.
>
> Then they asked me to receive some payments into my bank account and forward them on. For a small commission.
>
> My first paycheck never came. Then my bank froze my account. The packages had been bought with stolen cards, and the money I moved was stolen too. My name was on all of it.
>
> They didn't need me for my work. They needed my address and my bank account, so the trail would lead to me instead of them.
>
> If a job asks you to move packages or money for strangers, say no. It could make you a suspect in someone else's crime.

**Takeaway on screen:** Never move money or packages for someone you haven't met.
**Guidance:** FBI IC3 PSA on money mules: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2021/PSA211203 · FTC, Job scams: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/job-scams

## 10. Romance that turns into investing (pig butchering)

**Based on a case:** Hong Kong Hung Hom deepfake romance scam centre, 2023–2024; FTC romance scam guidance.
**Avatar brief:** a man in his forties or fifties with a phone in his hand, speaking softly, still hurt.

> She messaged me by mistake — wrong number, she said. We started talking. Every morning, good morning. Every night, sweet dreams. For weeks.
>
> She was successful. She traded crypto with her uncle's platform, and she wanted me to have a good future too. I started small. The app showed profits. So I put in more.
>
> When I tried to withdraw, there were fees. Then taxes. Then she stopped answering.
>
> Later I read about a scam centre in Hong Kong where the "women" were men using AI-generated photos and deepfakes, trained with scripts, and the victims lost hundreds of millions of dollars. People like me.
>
> She was never real. But the loneliness they found was.
>
> If someone you've never met in person starts guiding you to invest — stop, and tell someone you trust.

**Takeaway on screen:** Love and investment advice from a stranger online don't mix.
**Facts from:** The Standard, Hong Kong (2024-10-14); FTC, What to know about romance scams: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams. See `data/cases/hong-kong-hung-hom-deepfake-romance-ring.json`.

## 11. Deepfake of a famous person selling something

**Based on a case:** Gisele Bündchen deepfake ads, Brazil, 2024–2025 (arrests, no conviction reported).
**Avatar brief:** an older adult on a sofa scrolling a phone, speaking candidly.

> It was her. The supermodel. In a video on Instagram, talking about a skincare cream. Then another ad — she was giving away suitcases, you only paid for shipping.
>
> It was a small amount, so I paid. Nothing ever arrived.
>
> The video was fake. AI made her face and her voice. Police in Brazil arrested a group that had collected millions this way, a few dollars at a time. Most people never reported it, because they felt silly over such a small loss.
>
> I'm reporting it. That's how they get caught.
>
> Famous people don't give away things through random ads that ask you to pay. If a celebrity is selling you something in a video, look it up on their real, verified account first.

**Takeaway on screen:** A famous face in an ad is not proof. It can be AI.
**Facts from:** Reuters via Yahoo News (2025-10-03). See `data/cases/brazil-gisele-bundchen-deepfake-ads.json`.

## 12. A loved one's cloned voice in an emergency

**Based on official guidance:** FTC family emergency AI voice alert; FBI IC3 generative AI fraud PSA.
**Avatar brief:** a grandparent in a living room, shaken, speaking as if it happened yesterday.

> The phone rang at night. It was my grandson — his voice, crying. He'd had an accident, he was in trouble, he needed money for a lawyer. And he said, "Please, don't tell Mom."
>
> Then a man came on, said he was the lawyer, and told me how to send the money. Fast.
>
> My hands were shaking. I almost did it.
>
> Something made me call my daughter first. My grandson was at home, asleep.
>
> The voice was made by a computer, from a few seconds of him talking online.
>
> Now our family has a secret word. If someone calls in trouble and can't say it, I hang up and call them back on the number I know.
>
> Don't trust the voice. Call them back.

**Takeaway on screen:** Don't trust the voice. Hang up and call back.
**Guidance:** FTC, Scammers use AI to enhance their family emergency schemes (2023-03-20): https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes · FBI IC3 PSA (2024-12-03): https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203

## 13. Deepfake boss on a video call

**Based on a case:** Arup, Hong Kong, 2024.
**Avatar brief:** an office worker at a desk with a laptop, precise and professional, with a flash of disbelief.

> The email said it was from our chief financial officer in London. A confidential transaction. I had doubts — that's why they set up a video call.
>
> And there he was. The CFO. Other colleagues I knew. Their faces, their voices. They explained everything, and I made the transfers. Several of them. About twenty-five million dollars in total.
>
> None of the people on that call were real.
>
> I did what everyone tells you to do — I wanted to see their faces. But seeing a face isn't proof anymore.
>
> If anyone, even your boss, asks for an urgent or secret payment, hang up and call them back on a number you already have. And make sure no single person can move that much money alone.

**Takeaway on screen:** Seeing a face on a call is no longer proof.
**Facts from:** CFO Dive (2024-05-17); FBI IC3 PSA (2024-12-03). See `data/cases/arup-hong-kong-deepfake-video-call.json`.

## 14. Sextortion

**Based on official guidance:** FBI sextortion guidance.
**Avatar brief:** a teenager or young adult in a bedroom with low light, frightened at first and then relieved. Nothing sexual on screen. Consider a voice-only or animated version for younger audiences.

> She followed me on Instagram. Cute, my age, from a nearby town. We talked for a few days, and she sent a photo, and asked for one back.
>
> The second I sent it, everything changed. "Pay five hundred dollars or I send this to your family, your school, everyone on your followers list." They already had the list.
>
> I thought my life was over. I didn't want to tell anyone. That's exactly what they wanted.
>
> I told my older sister. She helped me stop answering, block them, save the messages, and report it. They didn't win.
>
> If this is happening to you: it's not your fault, don't pay, and tell an adult you trust. You are not in trouble. The person threatening you is the criminal.

**Takeaway on screen:** Don't pay. Tell someone. You're not in trouble.
**Guidance:** FBI, Sextortion: https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/scams-and-safety/common-scams-and-crimes/sextortion (this page blocks automated checks, so confirm the link by hand before publishing). Teens can also use NCMEC's Take It Down service to help remove images.

## 15. Rental scam

**Based on official guidance:** FTC, Rental listing scams.
**Avatar brief:** a young adult or new arrival in a city, sitting on a packed suitcase in a stairwell.

> I'd just moved for a new job. I found a perfect apartment online, cheaper than the others, close to work.
>
> The owner said he was working abroad, so I couldn't see it, but he'd mail me the keys once I paid the deposit and first month. There were other people interested, he said, so I had to be quick.
>
> I sent the money. The keys never came. When I went to the address, a family already lived there. They had never heard of him.
>
> I lost my savings and I had nowhere to sleep.
>
> If you can't see a place in person, and the owner wants money first — especially by wire or gift card — don't pay. Real landlords can show you the door.

**Takeaway on screen:** No viewing, no payment.
**Guidance:** FTC, Rental listing scams: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/rental-listing-scams

## Adding a script

1. Pick a case from `data/cases/`, or a pattern from official guidance.
2. Use only facts from the sources. Invent feelings and small everyday details, never facts about the case.
3. Keep it to about 120–180 words, write it to be spoken, and end with one practical action.
4. Fill in the "Based on", avatar brief, takeaway and sources lines.
